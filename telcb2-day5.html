01

Where you are now

You know the format of all four parts. That is most of the work.

The last week before an exam is not a learning week. Anything you try to learn now will not be stable enough to use under pressure, and the effort of cramming costs you more in sleep and calm than it gains you in knowledge.

So this week has three jobs: keep your German warm, make the format automatic, and arrive rested. That's all.

You need 60% in each block — 135 of 225 written, 45 of 75 oral. That means you can get roughly two out of every five things wrong and still pass comfortably. This exam is not looking for perfection, and neither should you.

02

The seven days

Keep sessions short — 60 to 90 minutes a day is plenty. Consistency matters more than volume this week.

7 days out
One full written mock, timedReading, Sprachbausteine, listening, writing — in one sitting, no pauses. Not to learn, but to find out how the time feels. Score it and then set it aside.
6 days out
Writing dayTwo letters: one Anfrage, one Beschwerde, 30 minutes each. Check only three things — Betreff present, three Leitpunkte developed, Sie-form throughout.
5 days out
Speaking Part 1Record yourself on three of the seven topics. Listen back once. You're checking length and whether the evaluation is there — not hunting for grammar mistakes.
4 days out
Speaking Parts 2 and 3With a partner if possible, out loud alone if not. Practise the two moves that matter: concede-then-object, and reaching agreement.
3 days out
Listening onlyThe audio plays once, so train that. Two or three practice sets. Accept that missing a few items is normal.
2 days out
Light reviewRead through your phrase lists aloud. Say them, don't stare at them. Half an hour, then stop.
1 day out
Almost nothingPack your bag, check the address and time, watch something in German for pleasure. No mock tests. Sleep early — this is the single highest-value thing you can do for your score.

If you fall behind this schedule, don't compress two days into one. Skip a day instead. Arriving tired costs more points than a missed practice session.

03

All four parts, briefly

If you remember nothing else, remember these four shapes.

Day 1 · Writing
Six blocks
Betreff → Anrede → Einleitung → Hauptteil → Forderung → Schlussformel.

Three Leitpunkte, properly. 150–200 words. Sie throughout.
Day 2 · Speaking 1
Five steps
Einstieg → Rahmen → Kern → Bewertung → Abschluss.

~90 seconds. Keywords, not a script. The Bewertung is the task.
Day 3 · Speaking 2
Four moves
These → Begründung → Beispiel → Rückfrage.

~20 seconds per turn. Concede, then object. End turns with a question.
Day 4 · Speaking 3
Point by point
Suggest → react → decide out loud → next point.

~50 seconds each. One compromise, quick agreement elsewhere.

The five phrases that carry the whole oral exam

Rückblickend würde ich sagen, dass …
Looking back I'd say that … — Part 1, the evaluation.
Da hast du zum Teil recht. Trotzdem sehe ich das anders, weil …
You're partly right. Still, I see it differently, because … — Part 2, the key move.
Wie siehst du das?
How do you see it? — Parts 2 and 3, keeps the interaction alive.
Können wir uns darauf einigen, dass …?
Can we agree that …? — Part 3, reaching the decision.
Fassen wir noch einmal zusammen: …
Let's summarise once more: … — the clean way to finish.
04

How to think about the examiners

Most exam nerves come from imagining the examiners as judges looking for faults. That is not what they are doing.

Two licensed examiners sit with you. They mostly moderate — they set the task, keep time, and observe. Your real conversation partner is the other candidate, not them. Their job is to fill in a criteria grid, and that grid is a list of things you can demonstrate, not a list of errors to hunt for.

Four things that are true, and worth knowing

  • They expect mistakes. B2 is defined as independent use, not accuracy. The written criterion is whether errors block understanding — not whether they exist.
  • An accent is fine. The pronunciation criterion asks for comprehensible speech with natural intonation. Nobody is scoring how German you sound.
  • You are graded individually. A dominant or a struggling partner cannot pull your score down. And helping a stuck partner is scored positively — it's exactly the interactive skill being tested.
  • They have heard hundreds of candidates. Your nervousness is completely ordinary to them. It is not being marked.

A useful reframe: the examiners are not deciding whether you're good enough. They're collecting evidence of what you can do — and every sentence you say gives them something to credit. Silence gives them nothing. That's the whole logic of the oral exam.

05

Mindset

Three swaps worth making

"I have to speak perfectly."
"I have to keep speaking." — fluency and task completion are worth more points than accuracy.
"I hope I get an easy topic."
"I have a structure for any topic." — you do. That's what the last four days were for.
"If I make a mistake, it's over."
"I can lose 40% and still pass." — which is the actual arithmetic.

When something goes wrong in the room

Something usually does. Here's what to do, and what to say.

You go blank
Moment, lass mich kurz überlegen.
One moment, let me think briefly. — a normal, human sentence. Two seconds of silence after it is fine.
A word is missing
Mir fällt das Wort gerade nicht ein — ich meine so etwas wie …
I can't think of the word — I mean something like … Paraphrasing scores better than stopping.
You didn't understand something
Entschuldigung, könnten Sie das bitte wiederholen?
Sorry, could you repeat that please? — asking is a competence, not a failure.
Your partner won't stop talking
Darf ich dazu kurz etwas sagen?
May I say something about that briefly? — polite interruption is a scored skill. Use it.
Your partner has gone quiet
Und wie ist das bei dir? Was würdest du vorschlagen?
And how is it for you? What would you suggest? — helping them helps you.

Steadying yourself

  • Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Four counts in, six out, a few times before you go in. It slows your heart rate; it is not a trick, it's physiology.
  • Speak your first sentence slowly. Rushing the opening is what makes people feel out of control. A slow first sentence sets the pace for everything after it.
  • Look at your partner, not the examiners. It makes the room feel smaller and the task feel like a conversation, which is what it is.
  • Let a bad section end. If the listening went badly, it's finished. Carrying it into the writing turns one weak section into two.
06

Exam day

The bag, the night before

  • ID / passport — the one you registered with
  • Registration confirmation, if your centre asks for it
  • Two or three pens that work
  • Water and something small to eat
  • The address, and how long the journey takes

The shape of the day

Roughly 140 minutes of written exam — reading and Sprachbausteine together, then listening, then writing. Then, at some point, 20 minutes of preparation for the oral exam, followed by about 15 minutes with your partner and two examiners.

In the 20-minute prep room, spend it roughly like this: 5 minutes on Part 1 (you prepared this at home), 8 minutes on Part 2 (read the text, pick a side, note two arguments), 5 minutes on Part 3 (one concrete idea per prompt). Two minutes spare.

Small things that help

  • Arrive 20–30 minutes early. Rushing is the most avoidable source of stress.
  • Eat something before. Two and a half hours of concentration on an empty stomach is unnecessarily hard.
  • Don't compare notes with other candidates between sections. It rarely helps and often unsettles.
  • If you get the chance in the prep room, agree with your partner to use du and decide who opens. It removes friction from the first thirty seconds.
07

Last word

You've done the preparation. In the room, your only job is to keep speaking, cover the points, and let the structures you practised do their work.

If a section goes badly, it is one block of many. If you don't pass a block, most centres let you retake just that block — it is not the end of anything.
